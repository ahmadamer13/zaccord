From the 45-Degree Rule to Nike's 3D Printing Lab

Two of the most common questions newcomers ask are "What is the 45-degree rule?" and "Does Nike really use 3D printing?" The answers highlight both the fundamentals of successful FDM printing and the cutting edge of footwear innovation.

Overhang control keeps FDM parts crisp even without heavy supports - photo: Wikimedia Commons

Understanding the 45-Degree Rule

In fused deposition modeling, plastic is laid down in horizontal layers. When each new layer sticks out beyond the previous one, you create an overhang. The 45-degree rule is a practical guideline stating that FDM printers can usually handle overhangs up to about forty-five degrees from vertical without needing support structures. Beyond that, gravity wins and freshly extruded filament droops because there is little material underneath to hold it.

  • Chamfer or fillet horizontal ledges so they rise at or below forty-five degrees instead of 90 degrees.
  • Split models into multiple pieces when reorientation avoids steep overhangs.
  • Slow print speed, increase cooling, or drop layer height for edge cases where you must push past the limit.

Treat the rule as a design guardrail rather than a hard law. With tuned cooling and materials like PLA or PETG, you can occasionally exceed it, but respecting forty-five degrees keeps prints faster and cleaner.

How Nike Uses 3D Printing

Yes, Nike relies on additive manufacturing extensively: first for prototyping and, increasingly, for performance parts. Flyprint uppers debuted in 2018 on Eliud Kipchoge's marathon shoes, using proprietary machines co-developed with HP's Multi Jet Fusion team. The lattice structures in those uppers allow Nike to tune breathability and flexibility zone by zone.

The company also collaborates with Carbon on photopolymer lattice midsoles, explores custom sprint spikes and cleat plates produced from athlete foot scans, and prints tooling inserts that accelerate mass-production runs. Additive manufacturing lets Nike test new traction patterns overnight, gather feedback from elite athletes the next day, and iterate again without waiting for injection molds.

For makers in Jordan, the takeaway is clear: master fundamentals like the 45-degree rule to deliver reliable parts, then look to Nike's example for how data-driven lattices and rapid prototyping open premium service offerings.
